# Lesson 0.1: The Robot Sandwich

**Time:** 30 min · **You need:** bread, a spread (jam, butter, cream cheese:
anything allergy-safe), a butter knife, a plate, paper and pencil.
No computer! · **New words:** instruction, program, computer

## Goal *(read this to the learner)*

> Today *you* are the programmer and *I* am the robot. Robots are VERY
> obedient, and VERY silly. They do exactly what you say. Not what you
> meant, what you *said*. Can you program me to make a sandwich?

## Before the lesson *(guide, 5 min)*

- [ ] Read this lesson once.
- [ ] Put the bread *in its bag*, the spread *with its lid on*, the knife
      and the plate on the table. Don't open anything!
- [ ] Practice your robot voice. Seriously, it helps.
- [ ] No sandwich allowed? Use "put on a jacket", "brush your teeth" or
      "build a tower of 5 blocks" instead. Same lesson.

## Warm-up (5 min)

Ask: "What's a computer? Where are there computers in our house?"
Let them list things: the laptop, phones, the TV, the microwave, the car,
maybe the washing machine. *(Most of these really do have small computers
inside.)* Say: "Every one of those computers only does what someone
told it to do. Today you'll find out how hard that is."

## Activity

1. **Say:** "Tell me, one step at a time, how to make a sandwich. I'll
   do *exactly* what you say."

2. **Be the robot.** Follow each instruction *literally*, and as
   unhelpfully as you can (safely):
   - "Put the bread on the plate." Put the whole *bag* on the plate.
   - "Open the bread." Pull at the bag helplessly: "HOW. DOES. ONE. OPEN."
   - "Put jam on the bread." Put the closed jar on top of the bread.
   - "Spread it with the knife." Hold the knife by the blade? No, keep
     it safe: hold it upside down, or wave it in the air above the bread.
   - "Take out two slices." Take out two slices and hold them forever,
     because you weren't told what to do next.

   Keep it fun. Let them laugh, groan, and fix their instructions.

3. **Say:** "Let's write it down this time, so we can get it right." The
   learner writes numbered steps on paper. Help with spelling if asked,
   but not with the steps.

4. **Run the program.** Read their steps out loud, one at a time, and
   do *exactly* what each one says. When a step fails, stop and say
   "ERROR on step 4!" in your robot voice.

5. **Fix and re-run.** They fix the broken step (crossing out and
   rewriting is fine), and you run it again *from step 1*. Repeat until
   the sandwich gets made.

6. Eat the sandwich. Programs that make snacks are the best programs.

## If something goes wrong

| What happens | What to do |
|---|---|
| They get frustrated with the silly robot | Ease off. Be silly about only one thing per step. Point out how good their fixes are. |
| They get upset when you "mess up" | Stop being the robot for a moment. "The robot isn't being mean. It just can't guess. That's why programmers have to be so clear." |
| It takes forever | Stop when the bread is out and has something on it. That counts. |

## Talk about it

- Why couldn't the robot just *figure out* what you meant?
  *(It doesn't know anything you don't tell it. It can't guess.)*
- What was the hardest step to explain? Why?
- A list of exact steps for a computer is called a **program**. Who
  writes the programs inside a microwave?
  *(People! Programmers wrote every one.)*

## Challenge *(if there's time, or for next time)*

Swap roles: you write instructions for drawing a simple picture (a house,
a smiley face) *without saying what it is*. The learner follows them
exactly, robot-style. Does the drawing come out right? Whose instructions
were the bug?

## Checkpoint

The learner can, without help:
- [ ] Explain why instructions for a computer must be exact
- [ ] Find a broken step in their own instructions and fix it

## Lab notebook

Help them set up the notebook: their name and "LAB NOTEBOOK" on the
cover. On the first page, write today's date and:
> A **program** is a list of exact instructions. Computers do exactly
> what the program says, even if it's silly.

Tape or copy in their final sandwich program.

## Guide's background *(optional reading)*

This is the most important idea in the whole course. Computers aren't
smart. They're incredibly fast and incredibly obedient. Every surprising
thing a computer does comes from a person writing exact instructions,
and every bug comes from instructions that said something slightly
different from what the person meant.

Professional programmers call this "the computer does what you *said*,
not what you *meant*." When your learner hits a frustrating bug years
from now, saying "Remember the sandwich robot?" will still help.
